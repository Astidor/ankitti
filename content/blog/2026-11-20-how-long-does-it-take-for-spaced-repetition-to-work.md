Spaced repetition starts working from your very first review: each time you pull an answer out of memory, it gets a little stronger. You'll usually notice it within a week or two, when cards you'd normally have forgotten are still there. The bigger payoff, where most of your cards stay with you for weeks at a time while you see them only now and then, takes about one to three months of steady daily study.

So the honest answer is "straight away, but you only see the full picture later". The rest of this post shows what happens at each stage, why the first weeks can feel like a lot of work for little reward, and how to check whether it's working for you.

## What "working" actually means

People mean three different things when they ask this question, and each one arrives at a different time.

- **You remember the card when it comes back.** This happens from day one, because the schedule shows you each card again before you're likely to forget it.
- **The gaps get long.** A card you answered a few times is now due in weeks or months, not days. This takes a few weeks per card.
- **The whole deck feels calm.** Your daily reviews stop climbing and settle at a steady number. This takes a couple of months.

If you judge the method by the third one in your first week, it will seem slow. If you judge it by the first, it's already working.

## Why the first review already helps

Two well-known findings explain why spaced repetition helps from the start.

The first is the forgetting curve. In experiments published in 1885, Hermann Ebbinghaus memorised lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself after different delays. He found that most forgetting happens soon after learning, and then slows down. That's why your first reviews come so close together: they catch a memory during the stretch when it fades fastest.

The second is the testing effect. Well-known experiments by Roediger and Karpicke (2006) showed that practising recall, rather than rereading, leads to better memory later on. Every flashcard review is a small act of recall, so even your first few reviews are doing useful work. The post on [spaced repetition vs rereading notes](/blog/spaced-repetition-vs-rereading-notes/) covers this in more detail.

What spaced repetition adds is timing. The [spacing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacing_effect) is the long-observed finding that study spread out over time beats the same amount of study crammed together. The scheduler handles the spreading for you.

## The timeline for one card

Here is what happens to a single new card in Ankitti if you answer **Good** every time, using the default settings. Instead of the gap between reviews, this table shows the day each review falls on, counting the day you first learn the card as day 0.

| Review | Day it happens | Time since you learned it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learning steps | Day 0 | a few minutes |
| 1st review | Day 1 | 1 day |
| 2nd review | Day 4 | 4 days |
| 3rd review | Day 12 | under 2 weeks |
| 4th review | Day 32 | about a month |
| 5th review | Day 82 | under 3 months |
| 6th review | Day 207 | about 7 months |

Look at how the reviews bunch up at the start. Four of the six reviews happen in the first month. After that, the card needs one review in the next two months and one more in the four months after that.

That's the main reason spaced repetition can feel slow at first. In the first few weeks, every card is still in its busy phase. The quiet phase only starts after a month or so, and that's when the method really pays you back.

The gaps grow because each Good multiplies the last gap by the card's ease, which starts at 2.5. The guide to [the SM-2 algorithm](/sm2-algorithm/) shows the full rules.

## The timeline for a whole deck

Now add new cards every day. Say you learn 10 new cards a day and, to keep the maths simple, answer Good on every review. Using the timeline above, here is roughly how many reviews you'd have each day:

| Point in time | Reviews a day |
| --- | --- |
| Days 1 to 3 | 10 |
| Day 4 to day 11 | 20 |
| Day 12 to day 31 | 30 |
| Day 32 to day 81 | 40 |
| Day 82 to day 206 | 50 |
| From day 207 | 60 |

Two things stand out.

First, your reviews climb fastest in the first month. Each new stage of the timeline adds another 10 reviews a day, and three of those stages arrive in the first two weeks.

Second, the climb slows down. Each stage adds the same 10 reviews, but each one arrives about two and a half times later than the one before. That's what "settling" looks like in practice.

This is the best case. In real life you'll forget some cards and press **Again**, which sends them back to short steps and adds extra reviews. Real numbers are usually higher, and the post on [how many flashcards to study a day](/blog/how-many-flashcards-a-day/) gives a more realistic range to plan with. The shape stays the same, though: a steep first month, then a much gentler slope.

## Why it can feel like it isn't working

If you're a few weeks in and doubting the method, one of these is usually the reason.

### You're judging it during the busy phase

In weeks two to four your reviews are growing and few cards have long gaps yet. It feels like constant repetition. Give it until the second month before you judge it.

### Too many new cards too soon

A big burst of new cards turns into a big wave of reviews a few days and weeks later. That wave can make the method feel like a chore. Fewer new cards, added every day, works better than lots of them now and then.

### Missed days

Reviews you skip don't disappear; they wait for you. A few missed days early on can leave you with a pile that hides the progress you've made.

### Cards that are too big

A card that asks for five facts at once gets half right a lot, so it keeps going back to the start. It never reaches the long gaps. Splitting it into smaller cards usually fixes this.

### Recognising instead of recalling

If you flip the card before really trying to answer, then press Good because the answer "looked familiar", the card moves on before you've learned it. It will come back later and catch you out. Try to say or think the answer before you flip.

### Pressing Hard on cards you knew

Hard shortens the next gap and lowers the card's ease. Done by habit, it keeps cards in the busy phase much longer than they need to be. The post on [the four answer buttons](/blog/should-you-press-hard-or-again-four-answer-buttons/) explains when each button fits.

## How to tell if it's working for you

You don't need special tools to check. A few simple signs are enough.

- **Older cards come back right.** When a card you learned two or three weeks ago comes up, you usually still know it. That's the clearest sign the method is doing its job.
- **Few cards keep failing.** A handful of stubborn cards is normal. If most of your reviews end in Again, the cards or the pace need changing, not the method.
- **You know things away from the app.** A word turns up in a film, or a fact comes to you in class, without a prompt. That's the point of all this.
- **Daily reviews start to level off.** After about two months of a steady number of new cards, the daily total should stop jumping up week after week.

If those signs aren't there after a month or two of steady study, look at your cards first. Clear, small cards that ask for one thing are what let the schedule do its work. The guide to [spaced repetition](/spaced-repetition/) has more on how to set yourself up well.

## The takeaway

Spaced repetition helps from the first review, you'll usually notice it within a week or two, and the full benefit shows after one to three months. The first month is the busiest, because every card is still in its short-gap phase. Keep your new cards steady, review every day, rate honestly, and give it until the second month before you decide how well it works for you.
