Your reviews pile up for one simple reason: more cards are becoming due each day than you are clearing. Usually that's because you added new cards faster than your daily time allows, missed a few days, or kept failing the same hard cards. The fix is to stop adding new cards, clear the backlog in small daily blocks, and then restart at a lower pace.

That's the short answer. The rest of this post looks at each cause in turn, because the right fix depends on which one is hitting you, and then gives you a plan you can start today.

## How a backlog actually grows

Every day, a spaced-repetition app does two things. It adds the new cards you asked for, and it brings back older cards whose waiting time has run out. If you finish everything that's due, you start tomorrow from zero. If you don't, today's leftovers sit on top of tomorrow's cards.

That's why a backlog can feel sudden. Nothing changes for weeks, then one busy week leaves a few hundred cards waiting, and the number looks too big to start. Most backlogs aren't a sign that spaced repetition has stopped working for you. They're a sign that the inputs and the time available have drifted apart.

## The five usual causes

### 1. Too many new cards, a few weeks ago

This is the most common one, and the hardest to spot, because the cost arrives late. A card you learn today comes back tomorrow, then a few days later, then a week or so after that. So the new cards you added in a burst three weeks ago are still showing up now.

If your reviews have grown steadily without you missing any days, this is almost certainly the reason. The earlier post on [how many flashcards to study a day](/blog/how-many-flashcards-a-day/) has a table for matching your new cards to the time you have.

### 2. Missed days add up, they don't pause

Spaced repetition doesn't stop the clock when you take a day off. If you usually clear 80 reviews a day and you skip three days, you come back to around 320 cards due, not 80. Skip a week and the number can look frightening, even though nothing has really gone wrong: those cards are simply waiting.

### 3. Forgotten cards start the short steps again

When you press **Again** on a review, the card doesn't just come back tomorrow. It goes back to short relearning steps (in Ankitti, a minute and then ten minutes; Anki works in a similar way) and its interval starts small again. Its ease also drops by 0.20, so its future gaps grow more slowly than before.

A handful of forgotten cards is normal and healthy. But if a large share of your reviews are failures, every one of them turns into several extra reviews over the next days.

### 4. Pressing Hard when you mean Good

**Hard** is meant for answers you got right with real effort. In SM-2 scheduling, Hard only stretches the gap by about 1.2 times and lowers the card's ease by 0.15. Pressed now and then, it's useful. Pressed out of habit on cards you knew, it keeps those cards coming back far more often than they need to. Over a few weeks that adds a steady extra load. If you want the details, the guide to [the SM-2 algorithm](/sm2-algorithm/) shows how each button changes the next interval.

### 5. Cards that are too big

A card that asks for a list of six side effects, or a full paragraph, is slow to review and easy to get partly wrong. Slow cards eat your time, and cards you keep half-failing come back constantly. Some of these are what Anki calls leeches: cards you've failed so many times that they cost far more than they're worth.

## A plan to clear the backlog

You don't need to delete your decks or reset your progress. Work through these steps in order.

### Step 1: stop adding new cards

Set new cards to zero until the backlog is gone. This is the single most effective change, because it stops the pile growing from the bottom while you clear it from the top. Your reviews usually start falling within a few days.

### Step 2: pick a daily amount you can finish

Look at how many reviews you can honestly clear in the time you have, and aim for that plus a little more each day. For example, if your normal load is about 80 and you have 400 waiting, doing 150 a day clears the extra in about a week while keeping up with the cards that fall due in the meantime.

Short blocks work better than one long sitting. Two sessions of 15 minutes are easier to start, and you'll answer more honestly when you're not tired.

### Step 3: answer honestly, even when it hurts

When you come back after a gap, you'll have forgotten some cards. Press **Again** on them. It's tempting to press Good on anything that feels familiar just to make the number go down, but that pushes cards you don't know weeks into the future, and you'll meet them again forgotten. Honest answers cost a little more now and save a lot later.

### Step 4: fix or remove the worst cards as you go

When a card makes you groan, deal with it instead of failing it for the fifth time:

- **Split it.** Turn one card with five facts into five small cards. One fact per card is faster to review and easier to remember.
- **Rewrite it.** Add a clue, an example or a picture so the answer has something to hang on.
- **Delete it.** If you don't really need to know it, let it go. A smaller deck you keep up with beats a large one you avoid.

### Step 5: restart new cards at a lower number

Once your daily reviews feel manageable for a few days in a row, bring new cards back at about half your old number. If that feels easy after two or three weeks, raise it a little.

## What not to do

- **Don't hide the problem with a review cap.** Anki has a maximum reviews per day setting, which is useful for keeping a single day bounded. But if you set it below what falls due, cards stay overdue and the backlog quietly grows behind the cap.
- **Don't reset the deck.** Starting over throws away everything the schedule has learned about which cards you know well. The cards you remember will clear quickly anyway.
- **Don't try to clear it all in one evening.** A three-hour session leads to tired, rushed answers, which leads to more failed cards next week.

## Keeping it from happening again

A few small habits make a big backlog much less likely:

- **Study a little every day.** Spaced repetition rewards showing up far more than it rewards long sessions.
- **Choose new cards for your bad days.** Set a number you could keep up during a busy week, not the one you'd manage on a quiet Sunday.
- **Plan breaks.** If you know a busy week is coming, stop new cards a week or two before. Your reviews shrink a little, and the gap will hurt less.
- **Keep cards small.** Most slow, often-failed cards are trying to hold too much.

If you'd like a refresher on why the schedule works the way it does, the guide on [what spaced repetition is](/spaced-repetition/) covers the basics.

## If you use Ankitti

A few things in Ankitti help while you catch up. The keyboard shortcuts (Space to flip, 1 to 4 to rate) make each review quicker, and the focus timer is handy for working in short blocks. The progress page has a 12-week calendar, so you can see at a glance whether you're studying most days. If your decks are in Anki, you can [open your .apkg files](/open-apkg-file/) with their review history and carry on where you left off.

One thing to avoid: **Practise anyway** lets you study a deck at any time without changing its schedule, which is great before a test, but it won't clear due cards. For the backlog, do your normal reviews.

## The short version

A backlog means more cards are falling due than you're clearing. Stop new cards, clear a steady amount each day in short blocks, answer honestly, and fix the cards that keep tripping you up. Then come back to new cards slowly. Your progress isn't lost; the cards are just waiting for you.
