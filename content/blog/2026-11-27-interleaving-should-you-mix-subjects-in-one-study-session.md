Usually, yes, but mix related topics rather than everything at once. Interleaving means switching between similar kinds of material in one session, for example mixing several types of maths problem or several verb tenses, instead of finishing one type before starting the next. It feels harder and slower, but it tends to help you tell similar things apart and pick the right method when the question doesn't tell you which chapter it came from.

This post explains what interleaving is, why it works, where it doesn't help much, and how to use it with flashcards and practice questions.

## What interleaving means

The usual way to practise is in blocks. You learn topic A and do ten questions on it, then topic B and ten questions on that, then topic C. Teachers often call this blocked practice. Textbooks are built this way too: each chapter ends with exercises that all use the method the chapter just taught.

Interleaving shuffles those blocks. After you've learned A, B and C, you practise them mixed: an A question, then a C, then a B, then another A, in no fixed order.

Two points are easy to miss:

- **Interleaving is about practice, not first learning.** You still meet each new idea on its own first. Mixing starts once you have something to mix.
- **It works best with related material.** Mixing three kinds of geometry problem is interleaving. Switching between French verbs and organic chemistry every few minutes is mostly just switching.

## Why mixing helps

### It makes you choose

In a block of identical questions, you know the method before you read the question. The real skill of spotting which method fits never gets practised. In an exam, questions arrive mixed, and that choosing step is often where marks are lost. Interleaved practice trains it directly.

### It shows you the differences

When similar things appear side by side, you start noticing what separates them. Two well-known studies show this. Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor (2007) found that students who practised mixed sets of maths problems did better on a later test than students who practised the same problems in blocks, even though the mixed practice felt harder at the time. Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork (2008) had people learn the painting styles of different artists. Seeing the artists' paintings mixed together helped people recognise new paintings by the same artists better than seeing each artist's work in a block, and most participants still believed the blocked version had worked better.

That last detail matters. Interleaving usually feels worse while you're doing it. You get more wrong and feel less fluent. That feeling isn't a sign it's failing.

### It adds a little spacing

When you mix topics, each one comes back after a short gap instead of straight away. That gap means a bit of forgetting, so each return takes more effort. This is closely related to the [spacing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacing_effect), and it's one reason the two ideas are often mentioned together.

## When interleaving doesn't help much

Interleaving isn't a rule for every situation.

- **Brand new material.** If you don't yet understand a topic, mixing it with others just adds confusion. Learn it on its own first, with a few blocked examples, and start mixing once it makes sense.
- **Unrelated subjects.** The main benefit comes from telling similar things apart. History dates and Spanish vocabulary don't compete with each other, so mixing them gives you little beyond a change of scene.
- **Very short sessions.** If you only have five minutes, finishing one small set is fine. Mixing pays off over a whole week of practice, not inside every single session.
- **When you need one skill fast.** If tomorrow's test covers one topic only, practise that topic.

A sensible pattern is block first, then mix: learn a new topic in a focused block, then fold it into mixed practice with the topics it could be confused with.

## How to interleave with flashcards

Spaced repetition already does some of this for you. A review session usually brings up cards you learned on different days, in a mixed order, so within one deck you're interleaving without trying. The questions worth thinking about are how you split your decks and how you order your sessions.

### Keep confusable things in the same deck

If two ideas are easy to mix up, you want them to turn up near each other. Drug classes with similar names, Spanish *ser* and *estar*, the different kinds of triangle: put them in one deck so a normal review session mixes them. Splitting every small topic into its own deck works against this, because each session then becomes a block.

A deck per subject or per course is usually a good size. A deck per lecture or per chapter often turns into blocked practice by accident.

### Write cards that ask for the difference

Some confusions survive mixing because each card only ever asks about one item. Add a card that compares them directly:

| Instead of only | Also add |
| --- | --- |
| "What does *ser* mean?" | "When do you use *ser* rather than *estar*?" |
| "Formula for the area of a triangle?" | "Area of a triangle vs a parallelogram: what's the difference?" |
| "What class is drug X?" | "Drug X and drug Y have similar names. How do their classes differ?" |

These cards are often the most useful in a deck, because they target exactly the moment you'd hesitate.

### Alternate decks within a session

If you have several related decks, say vocabulary and grammar for the same language, you don't have to finish one completely before opening the other. Doing a short round of each and then going back can keep both fresh. With unrelated subjects, finishing one deck and then the next is fine, as mixing those gives little extra.

## How to interleave practice questions

Flashcards handle facts well. For skills like solving problems, interleaving matters even more.

1. **Learn the new method in a block.** Work through a few examples of the new type until you can do one without help.
2. **Make a mixed set.** Take questions from the last few chapters or topics, not just the newest. Cover up the chapter headings if you can.
3. **Name the method before solving.** For each question, decide first which method or rule applies. This is the step blocked practice skips.
4. **Check, then note what fooled you.** If you picked the wrong method, write down what made the two look alike. That note can become a comparison flashcard.
5. **Mix older topics back in.** Each week, include a few questions from earlier material so it doesn't fade while you focus on the new.

Past papers are already interleaved, which is one reason they're such good practice once you've covered the course.

## The takeaway

Learn new things on their own first, then mix them with the topics they could be confused with. Keep confusable material in the same deck, add cards that ask for the difference, and use mixed sets of practice questions where you name the method before solving. It will feel slower and messier than blocked practice. That's normal, and it's often a sign the practice is doing its job.
