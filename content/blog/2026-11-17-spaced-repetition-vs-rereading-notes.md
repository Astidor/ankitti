For remembering things over weeks and months, spaced repetition works better than rereading. Rereading makes material feel familiar, but it doesn't make you practise pulling it out of your memory, which is what an exam or a conversation asks of you. Rereading still has a place: it's useful the first time you meet a topic, and for seeing how ideas fit together. After that, testing yourself on a schedule is the better use of your time.

The rest of this post explains why the two feel so different from what they actually do, and how to use each one for the job it's good at.

## What each method really asks of you

When you **reread**, your notes do the work. The words are in front of you, and your brain only has to recognise them. That's pleasant and fast, which is exactly why most of us default to it.

When you use **spaced repetition**, you see a question and have to produce the answer before you look. Then the card comes back after a gap that grows each time you get it right. Two things are going on:

- **Retrieval**: you pull the answer out of memory instead of reading it. This is often called active recall, or retrieval practice.
- **Spacing**: the reviews are spread out over days and weeks instead of bunched into one sitting.

Each of these helps memory on its own. Spaced repetition puts them together, and rereading usually has neither: you aren't retrieving anything, and rereading tends to happen in one block shortly before you need it.

## What the research says, in plain terms

Two findings from memory research matter most here, and both are well established.

### The testing effect

Trying to recall something strengthens your memory of it more than looking at it again for the same amount of time. A well-known study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke (2006) had students read short passages, then either reread them or try to recall them. On a test a few minutes later, the rereaders did slightly better. On tests days later, the students who had practised recalling did clearly better.

That pattern is worth remembering, because it explains why rereading is so convincing. It really does help in the very short term. It just fades faster.

### The spacing effect

The same amount of study, spread out over time, leads to better long-term memory than one long session. This goes back to Hermann Ebbinghaus's experiments in 1885, where he tested his own memory of nonsense syllables and found that spreading out his practice helped him relearn lists with less effort. It has been repeated many times since. If you'd like an overview, Wikipedia's article on the [spacing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacing_effect) is a fair starting point.

## Why rereading feels like it works

If rereading is weaker, why does it feel so good? Because it gives you the wrong signal.

When you read a page for the third time, it flows. You know what comes next. That ease feels like knowing the material. But recognising something when it's in front of you is a different skill from recalling it when it isn't. Think of a face you'd recognise anywhere but whose name you can't bring up: the memory is there, but you can't get to it on demand.

Self-testing gives you an honest signal instead. If you can't answer a card, you find out now, while there's still time to do something about it, not in the exam hall.

Some other common traps of rereading:

- **It's easy to drift.** Your eyes keep moving while your mind is somewhere else, and you can finish a chapter without having thought about any of it.
- **It treats everything the same.** You spend as long on the parts you already know as on the parts you don't.
- **It tends to get crammed.** Rereading usually happens the night before, which is the opposite of spacing.

## Where rereading still earns its place

None of this means you should never open your notes again. Rereading is the right tool in a few situations:

- **The first time through.** You can't test yourself on something you haven't understood. Read, listen to the lecture, work through the examples first.
- **Seeing the big picture.** Flashcards are good at single facts. Rereading a chapter summary shows you how those facts connect, which helps you make sense of them.
- **After a failed card.** When you get a card wrong, going back to the source for a minute to see the fact in context often makes it stick better next time.
- **Material you only need once.** If you need to recall something tomorrow and never again, a quick reread may be all it's worth.

The useful question isn't "which method is better?" but "what job am I doing right now?" Understanding comes from reading. Remembering comes from recall, spaced out over time.

## How the schedule spreads out your effort

Here is what spaced repetition looks like for one card, using the default settings in Ankitti, which follow the same SM-2 style of scheduling as Anki. If you answer **Good** each time, the card comes back roughly like this:

| Review | Comes back after |
| --- | --- |
| First time you see it | 10 minutes |
| Second look | 1 day |
| Third | 3 days |
| Fourth | 8 days |
| Fifth | 20 days |
| Sixth | 50 days |

That's six short reviews spread over about three months, each one a few seconds long. Compare that with rereading a set of notes three times in the week before an exam: more time spent, all of it bunched together, and none of it practising recall.

The gaps grow because each successful recall makes the memory more durable, so it can safely wait longer next time. If you forget a card, it drops back to short steps and comes back more often until you have it again. ## A simple way to combine the two

You don't have to choose one method forever. A routine that uses both might look like this:

1. **Read first, once, carefully.** Go through the lecture or chapter to understand it. Don't highlight everything; just follow the ideas.
2. **Make a few cards the same day.** Pick the facts and ideas you'll need to recall. Keep each card to one question. Ten good cards beat forty vague ones.
3. **Close the notes and review.** Do your due cards every day. Answer from memory before you flip, and rate yourself honestly.
4. **Go back to the source only when a card fails.** Use the notes to repair what you've forgotten, not to reread everything.
5. **Reread summaries now and then.** Once a week or so, skim a chapter summary to keep the bigger picture in view.

How much time to give step 3 depends on how many new cards you add. The post on [how many flashcards to study a day](/blog/how-many-flashcards-a-day/) has a table to help you pick a number you can keep up.

## The takeaway

Rereading helps you understand; recalling helps you remember. Read new material once to make sense of it, then turn the important parts into cards and let spaced repetition bring them back just as you're about to forget.
