For most people, **10 to 20 new cards a day** is the right place to start. That sounds small, but every new card you add today comes back several times over the next weeks, so your daily reviews grow to several times your new cards. Pick a number of new cards you could manage on a busy day, not on your best day, and only raise it once the reviews feel easy.

The rest of this post explains why the number matters so much, how to work out what your own limit is, and what to do if you've already taken on too much.

## New cards and reviews are two different numbers

When you study with spaced repetition, each day has two kinds of cards:

- **New cards**: ones you've never seen. You choose how many of these to add.
- **Reviews**: cards you've learned before that are due again today. You don't choose these; the schedule does.

The trap is that you only control the first number, but the second one is where your time goes. Adding 50 new cards on an enthusiastic Sunday feels productive. Two weeks later, those 50 cards are still coming back, mixed in with everything you added since.

## How one new card turns into several reviews

Here is what happens to a single card in Ankitti if you answer **Good** every time, using the default settings (the same style of scheduling Anki uses):

| Review | Comes back after |
| --- | --- |
| First time you see it | 10 minutes |
| Second look | 1 day |
| Third | 3 days |
| Fourth | 8 days |
| Fifth | 20 days |
| Sixth | 50 days |

So a card you learn on day 0 is due again on roughly day 1, day 4, day 12, day 32 and day 82. If you add the same number of new cards every day, then on any given day you'll be reviewing cards you learned 1, 4, 12, 32 and 82 days ago. That's about **five reviews for every new card you add each day**, even if you never forget anything.

You will forget things, and that's normal. A card you miss goes back to the short steps and returns more often for a while, which adds to the pile. So a fair rule of thumb is to expect your daily reviews to settle at **five to eight times your daily new cards** after a couple of months.

If you want to see the maths behind those gaps, the guide to [the SM-2 algorithm](/sm2-algorithm/) walks through it with real numbers.

## A table to plan with

Reviews are quick. Most take a few seconds once a card is familiar. If you allow around 10 seconds per card, here is roughly what different daily new-card numbers lead to once things settle:

| New cards a day | Reviews a day (approx.) | Time a day (approx.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 25–40 | 5–7 minutes |
| 10 | 50–80 | 10–15 minutes |
| 20 | 100–160 | 20–30 minutes |
| 40 | 200–320 | 40–60 minutes |

These are estimates, not promises. Hard material, long answers and cards you keep missing all take longer. But the shape holds: **doubling your new cards roughly doubles your daily time**, and it does so a few weeks later, when the extra load is hardest to notice coming.

## How to choose your number

Ask yourself three questions.

### How much time do you really have on a bad day?

Not a good day. A day with a late shift, a cold or a long commute. If the honest answer is 15 minutes, start at around 10 new cards. Spaced repetition rewards showing up every day far more than it rewards big sessions, so a number you can keep beats a number that impresses you.

### How hard are the cards?

Ten new kanji with readings and example words are a heavier load than ten Spanish greetings. Dense cards, such as a drug with its class, mechanism and side effects, take longer to review and are forgotten more often. If your cards are dense, start lower, or split them into smaller cards (one fact per card is easier to remember and faster to review).

### Is there a deadline?

If an exam is coming, you can work backwards. Count the cards you need to learn, divide by the days you have, and leave the last week or two for reviews only. If the result is far above what fits in your day, that's a sign to cut the material down to what matters most, not to add 100 new cards a day and hope.

## Signs you've taken on too many

- Your reviews take noticeably longer each week.
- You start skipping days because the queue looks too big.
- You press **Good** on cards you didn't really remember, just to get through.

The last one matters most. Spaced repetition only works when your answers are honest. If you're rushing, the schedule shows you cards too rarely and you forget them anyway.

## How to catch up without starting over

If the pile has already grown too big, don't delete your decks.

1. **Stop adding new cards** for a few days. Your reviews will start shrinking almost straight away.
2. **Do the reviews in short blocks**, such as two sessions of 15 minutes, instead of one long sitting.
3. **Restart new cards at a lower number** than before, say half, once your daily reviews feel manageable again.

If you're coming back after a long break, it's fine to spread the backlog over a week. The cards will wait.

## Setting it up in Ankitti

In Ankitti, open a deck's menu, choose **Rename and settings**, and set **New cards a day** under daily limits. Your home screen shows how many cards are due today and roughly how long they'll take, so you can see straight away whether a number is working. If you want extra practice before a test, **Practise anyway** lets you go through a deck again without changing when its cards are due.

New to all this? Start with [what spaced repetition is](/spaced-repetition/), and if you already have Anki decks, you can [open your .apkg files](/open-apkg-file/) and keep your progress.

## The short version

Start with 10 to 20 new cards a day. Expect your reviews to settle at several times that number. Keep the amount you could manage on a bad day, study every day, and only add more when your reviews feel easy. Small and steady wins.
