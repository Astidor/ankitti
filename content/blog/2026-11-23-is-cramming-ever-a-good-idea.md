Cramming can work for one thing: getting through a test that is very close, on material you will never need again. It does lift your score in the short term. What it doesn't do is build memories that last, so anything you cram is mostly gone within days, and you'll have to learn it again if it comes up later. If you need the knowledge beyond the exam, spacing your study out is the better deal.

The rest of this post looks at when cramming is a reasonable choice, what it costs, and how to do it as well as possible when you have no other option.

## What cramming actually is

Cramming means packing most of your study into one long session, or a few, just before you need the material. Researchers call this massed practice, as opposed to spaced practice, where the same amount of study is spread over days or weeks.

The key point is that cramming isn't about how much time you spend. Two students can each give a topic six hours. One spends all six hours the night before. The other spends an hour on six different days. Same effort, very different results a month later.

## Why cramming feels like it works

Cramming has a real upside, which is why so many people rely on it. Material you studied a few hours ago is fresh. You recognise it, it comes to mind quickly, and on a test the next morning it often does come back.

The problem is how fast it goes. Hermann Ebbinghaus's experiments in 1885 showed that we forget new material quickly at first, and then more slowly. A crammed fact has had one burst of attention and no chance to be recalled again later, so it sits on the steep part of that curve. The well-known study by Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke (2006) showed a similar pattern: methods that look best on a test a few minutes later are not always the ones that hold up days later.

So cramming gives you a good result at exactly one moment, and not much after that. Whether that's a problem depends on what you need the knowledge for.

## When cramming is a fair choice

There are honest situations where a short, intense session is the sensible thing to do:

- **The material really is one-off.** A quiz on a reading you'll never return to, a set of names for a single meeting, a short test in a subject you're dropping.
- **You've run out of time.** If the exam is tomorrow and you haven't started, spacing isn't an option any more. A focused session tonight beats doing nothing.
- **It's a final pass, not the first one.** A review session the day before an exam, on material you've already studied over weeks, isn't really cramming. It's topping up memories that are already solid, and it works well.

The last case is the one worth aiming for. The trouble with cramming isn't studying hard before an exam. It's studying *only* before an exam.

## What cramming costs you

If the material matters beyond the test, cramming is expensive in a few quiet ways.

### You pay for the same knowledge twice

Courses build on themselves. If you cram for a mid-term and forget most of it, you'll have to learn it again for the final, or for next year's course that assumes you know it. Spaced study costs about the same time once and keeps paying off.

### It squeezes out sleep

Sleep plays an important part in turning what you studied into lasting memory, and a tired brain is slower at recalling things. Staying up late to cram can cost you some of the very performance you were cramming for. If you must choose between another hour of study and a proper night's sleep before an exam, sleep is usually the better bet.

### It hides what you don't know

A long session of reading over your notes makes everything feel familiar. That feeling isn't the same as being able to recall it. The post on [spaced repetition vs rereading notes](/blog/spaced-repetition-vs-rereading-notes/) explains why familiarity is such a misleading signal.

## How to cram well if you have to

If you're up against the clock, a few habits make a crammed session much more useful.

1. **Test yourself, don't reread.** Turn the main points into questions, or use cards you already have, and answer from memory before checking. Retrieval is the most useful thing you can do with limited time.
2. **Space within the evening.** Even a few hours give you room to spread things out. Go through your questions once, take a short break, go through the ones you missed, then come back to everything an hour or two later. A second look after a gap beats two looks in a row.
3. **Start with what's most likely to come up.** Past papers, the topics the teacher stressed, and anything you got wrong before. Don't spend your best hour on what you already know.
4. **Keep sessions short.** Work in blocks of 25 to 45 minutes with a few minutes off between them. Your attention drops in long, unbroken sessions, even if you keep your eyes on the page.
5. **Stop in time to sleep.** Finish with one quick pass over the cards you found hardest, then go to bed.
6. **Do a short review in the morning.** Ten or fifteen minutes on the hardest questions before the exam catches anything that slipped overnight.

## The better plan: space first, then do a final review

The most useful way to think about cramming is as the last step of a longer plan, not the whole plan. Here's how much spacing you can fit in, depending on how early you start.

The table uses Ankitti's default schedule, which follows Anki-style SM-2: a new card is seen again after 10 minutes, then after 1 day, and after that each gap is roughly 2.5 times the last when you press **Good**.

| You start | Reviews of a card before the exam (answering Good) |
| --- | --- |
| The night before | 1 learning session only |
| 1 week before | Day 0, then day 1 and day 4 |
| 2 weeks before | Day 0, then days 1, 4 and 12 |
| 1 month before | Day 0, then days 1, 4, 12 and about 32 |

Cards you forget come back sooner, so the real numbers vary, but the pattern is clear. Starting a week early already gives each card a few spaced reviews. Starting a month early means most cards have been recalled several times, with longer and longer gaps, by the time you sit down in the exam hall.

Then, in the last day or two, you can still do a focused run through the whole deck. That's the good kind of last-minute study: it refreshes memories that already exist instead of building them from nothing. ## The takeaway

Cramming is a fair tool for a test that's very close, on material you won't need again. For anything you want to keep, it's a costly way to study: it fades fast, eats into sleep and has to be repeated later. Start a little earlier, let the reviews spread out, and save the intense session for a final review of things you already know.
