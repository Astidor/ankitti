You can take a break from flashcards without losing much. Cards you know well have long gaps between reviews, so a week or two away barely touches them. What suffers is the recent material on short intervals, and the real cost is a pile of due reviews waiting when you return. Stop adding new cards a week or two before you go, clear what's due on your last day, and come back with a plan instead of a guilty three-hour session.

The rest of this post explains what happens to your deck while you're away, how to prepare, what to do during the break if you want to, and how to get back into a rhythm afterwards.

## What happens to your cards while you're away

Spaced-repetition apps don't stop the clock when you stop studying. Each card has a due date, and those dates keep arriving whether or not you open the app. So a break doesn't delete anything. It just means cards keep becoming due, and they wait for you.

How much a break hurts depends on where each card is in its schedule. With Anki-style SM-2 scheduling, the kind Ankitti uses, a card you answer **Good** each time comes back after 1 day, then about 3 days, then about 8, then about 20, then about 50, with each gap roughly 2.5 times the last. That gives you three rough groups:

| Where the card is | Typical gap | Effect of a two-week break |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New or just learned | Minutes to a few days | Often forgotten; will need relearning |
| A few weeks old | 1 to 3 weeks | Overdue; many will still come back with a little effort |
| Mature | Months | Probably not even due yet; usually untouched |

This matches what Hermann Ebbinghaus found in his forgetting-curve experiments in 1885: we forget quickly at first and then more slowly. Each successful review flattens the curve a little, which is why a well-reviewed card can survive a gap that would wipe out something you learned last Tuesday. For more on why this works, see the guide to [spaced repetition](/spaced-repetition/).

So the honest answer is that your older knowledge is mostly safe, your newest cards are at risk, and your first few days back will be busier than usual.

## Before the break: make the gap smaller

A little preparation makes the return much easier. You don't need to do all of these, but each one helps.

### Stop new cards a week or two early

New cards are the ones that need the most attention in their first days and weeks. If you keep adding them right up to the day you leave, you'll go away with a large batch of fragile cards on short intervals, exactly the ones a break hurts most.

Set new cards to zero about one to two weeks before the break. Your recent cards then have time to move onto longer intervals, and your daily reviews shrink a little before you go. If you're not sure how many new cards you normally take on, the post on [how many flashcards to study a day](/blog/how-many-flashcards-a-day/) has a simple way to work it out.

### Clear everything due on your last day

Start the break from zero. If you leave with a backlog already, it simply adds to the one that builds while you're away.

### Decide now what "coming back" looks like

Pick the day you'll restart and block out a bit more time than usual for the first few days. Knowing you have a plan makes it far less likely that a two-week break quietly turns into two months.

## During the break: a tiny habit, or none at all

You have two reasonable choices here, and both are fine.

**Take a full break.** If you're on holiday, ill, or just worn out, a real rest is worth more than a handful of reviews. Your deck will be there when you get back. Don't spend the break feeling guilty about it.

**Keep a five-minute habit.** If you have a phone and a few spare minutes, doing even a small part of each day's reviews keeps the pile from growing as fast. Aim for something you could do in a queue or on a train, not a full session. If you do this, keep new cards at zero; the point is to hold your ground, not to move forward.

What doesn't work well is the middle path: skipping several days, then doing one huge session, then skipping again. Long irregular sessions lead to tired, rushed answers, and rushed answers lead to more forgotten cards later.

## Coming back: a calm plan for your first week

When you return, the due count may look alarming. Remember that much of it is cards that are simply late, not cards you've lost. Here's a steady way through.

1. **Keep new cards at zero.** Don't add anything until the backlog is gone. This is the single most useful step.
2. **Set a daily target you can finish.** Take your normal daily review count and add about half again. If you usually do 80 reviews and 500 are waiting, doing 120 a day clears the extra in roughly two weeks while keeping up with what falls due.
3. **Work in short blocks.** Two or three sessions of 15 minutes are easier to start and keep your answers honest. One long evening session is where mistakes creep in.
4. **Answer honestly.** You will have forgotten some cards, especially recent ones. Press **Again** on those. Pressing **Good** on something that only feels familiar pushes it weeks away, and you'll meet it again forgotten. If you need a reminder of what each button does, see [should you press Hard or Again?](/blog/should-you-press-hard-or-again-four-answer-buttons/)
5. **Bring new cards back slowly.** Once your daily reviews feel normal for a few days in a row, restart new cards at about half your old number, then raise it again after a couple of weeks if it feels easy.

If the pile is very large, or you've been away for months, the post on [why reviews pile up and how to get back on track](/blog/why-anki-reviews-pile-up-and-how-to-get-back-on-track/) goes into more detail on clearing a big backlog.

## Things to avoid

- **Don't reset your deck.** It's tempting to wipe the slate clean after a long gap, but that throws away everything the schedule knows about which cards you remember well. Your mature cards will clear quickly anyway, often with a single glance each.
- **Don't delete the overdue cards.** Late isn't the same as lost. Most of them only need one review to get back on track.
- **Don't rate cards higher to make the number go down.** It feels like progress for a day and costs you more over the following weeks.
- **Don't judge yourself by the first day back.** Your first sessions will have more misses than usual. That's the schedule doing its job, catching the cards that slipped.

## If you use Ankitti

Ankitti works offline and has an Android app, so if you choose the five-minute habit during a trip, you can do a few reviews without a connection. Coming back, the keyboard shortcuts (Space to flip, 1 to 4 to rate) make a long review list go faster, and the focus timer helps you keep to short blocks. The progress page has a 12-week calendar, which is a quiet way to see when you stopped and how you're getting back into a routine.

One note: **Practise anyway** lets you go through any deck without changing its schedule. It's useful for a quick refresher, but it doesn't clear cards that are due, so for catching up after a break, do your normal reviews.

## The takeaway

A break from flashcards costs you a busy week, not your progress. Older cards are mostly safe, and recent ones may need relearning. Stop new cards before you go, start the break with nothing due, and when you come back, clear the pile in short daily blocks with honest answers before adding anything new. Rest is part of studying for the long term, and your deck will still be there when you're ready.
