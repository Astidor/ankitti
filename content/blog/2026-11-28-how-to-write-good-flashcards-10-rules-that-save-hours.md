A good flashcard asks one clear question that has one short answer, and you understand that answer before you try to memorise it. Most of the other rules follow from that: keep cards small, make the question precise, give enough context to tell similar cards apart, and only add what you'll actually need. Cards written this way take a few seconds to review and are rarely forgotten, which is where the hours are saved.

This post explains why card quality matters so much, then gives ten rules with examples, and ends with a checklist you can run through before saving a card.

## Why the way you write a card matters

With spaced repetition, a card you remember well is shown less and less often. In an Anki-style SM-2 schedule, a card you keep answering with **Good** goes out to roughly 1 day, then about 3 days, then about a week, then a few weeks, then a couple of months, with each gap about 2.5 times the last. In its first year, a well-made card might only need around six reviews after its first day.

A badly made card behaves very differently. Every time you forget it, it goes back to short gaps and starts climbing again. A card that you forget every few weeks can easily be reviewed many times more than a good one, and each of those reviews is slower because you're struggling with it.

So the real cost of a card isn't the minute it takes to write. It's every review that follows. A few extra seconds spent writing a clear card is the best trade in studying. The earlier post on [what to do when you keep forgetting the same card](/blog/what-to-do-when-you-keep-forgetting-the-same-card/) covers fixing cards after the fact. This one is about writing them well from the start.

## The 10 rules

### 1. Understand it before you make a card

Flashcards are good at keeping things you've understood. They are poor at teaching something you don't understand yet. If you can't explain an idea in your own words, go back to the textbook, lecture or a worked example first. Memorising a sentence you don't follow gives you a string of words you can repeat but can't use.

### 2. One fact per card

This is often called the minimum information principle. Piotr Woźniak, who created the SuperMemo software and the SM-2 algorithm, made it the centre of his well-known advice on formulating knowledge for spaced repetition. The idea is simple: a small card is either remembered or not, so your rating is honest and the schedule fits that fact. A big card is usually half remembered, and the parts you know drag along the parts you don't.

If you find yourself writing "and" in the answer, you probably have two cards.

### 3. Ask a question with one right answer

"Tell me about the French Revolution" has no clear answer, so you can't tell whether you got it right. "In what year did the storming of the Bastille happen?" does. Vague questions lead to generous ratings, and generous ratings send weak memories out to long gaps they aren't ready for.

A useful test: would two people who know the subject give the same answer? If not, make the question narrower.

### 4. Keep the answer short

The back of a card should be a word, a number, a name or a short phrase. If the answer is a paragraph, you'll end up reading it and nodding instead of recalling it. Put the short answer first and, if you want, a line of extra explanation underneath that you don't have to recall.

### 5. Use cloze cards for facts that live inside a sentence

Some facts are easiest to learn in context. A cloze card hides one part of a sentence and asks you to fill it in, for example: "The mitochondria produce most of the cell's {{c1::ATP}}." Cloze works well for definitions, key terms and sentences in a new language.

Two cautions. Hide one thing per card, not three. And make sure the rest of the sentence doesn't give the answer away by grammar alone.

### 6. Break lists into pieces

Lists are the hardest thing to learn on a single card. "Name the eight parts of speech" will fail again and again, because forgetting one item counts as a miss. Better options:

- One card per item, each asking what makes that item different.
- Cloze cards that hide one item at a time from the full list.
- A short memory hook for the order, if the order matters.

If you really need to recite a whole list, learn the pieces first and practise the full list separately.

### 7. Add context so similar cards don't collide

Cards that look alike get confused. "What does *largo* mean?" has a different answer in Spanish and Italian. A short label at the start of the question fixes this: "Spanish: *largo*" or "Music: *largo*". The same goes for drug names, dates and formulas that resemble each other. If two cards keep getting mixed up, add a third card that asks directly for the difference. The post on [interleaving](/blog/interleaving-should-you-mix-subjects-in-one-study-session/) has more on comparison cards.

### 8. Use your own words and examples

Copying a sentence from a textbook is quick, but your own wording is easier to remember, and writing it forces you to check that you understand it. A concrete example often helps more than a definition. "What's an example of a primary source for the history of the First World War?" is easier to hold onto than an abstract definition of "primary source".

### 9. Add a picture when the thing is visual

Anatomy, maps, chemical structures, artworks, road signs and many kinds of diagram are easier to learn from an image than from words. A picture can also act as a memory hook for an abstract word. Keep it simple, though: one clear image that matches the question, not a busy slide where the answer is hard to find.

### 10. Only make reverse cards when you need both directions

It's tempting to make every card work both ways. For vocabulary you want to read and speak, that can be worth it. For most facts, it doubles your reviews for little benefit. Ask whether you'll ever need to go from the answer back to the question. If not, one direction is enough.

## Before and after

Here are some common card problems and simple rewrites.

| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| Q: "Photosynthesis?" A: a paragraph | Q: "What gas do plants take in for photosynthesis?" A: "Carbon dioxide" (and a few more small cards) |
| Q: "List the causes of the First World War" | One card per cause, each asking how it contributed |
| Q: "*Banco*?" | Q: "Spanish: *banco* (two meanings)" A: "bank; bench" |
| Q: "What is a for loop?" A: textbook definition | Q: "In Python, what does `for x in range(3)` loop over?" A: "0, 1, 2" |
| Cloze: "{{c1::Paris}} is the {{c1::capital}} of {{c1::France}}" | Cloze: "Paris is the capital of {{c1::France}}" |

Notice that the "after" versions usually mean more cards, not fewer. That's fine. Five small cards that each take three seconds are much cheaper to keep than one big card you fail every fortnight.

## A quick checklist before you save a card

Run through these in a few seconds:

1. Do I understand this?
2. Is there only one thing to recall?
3. Would someone who knows the subject give the same answer?
4. Is the answer short enough to say in one breath?
5. Could this card be confused with another one? If so, is there a label?
6. Will I actually need this?

The last question matters more than it seems. Every card costs you reviews for months. If a fact is trivia you'll never use, leaving it out is a perfectly good choice. If you find a card annoying weeks later, rewrite it or delete it. Your deck should get better over time, not just bigger.

## The takeaway

Write small, clear cards about things you understand. One fact, one precise question, one short answer, with enough context to keep similar cards apart. It takes slightly longer to write each card, but you'll forget fewer of them and spend far less time on reviews.
